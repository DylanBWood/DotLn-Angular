What the studies alone taught the verifier:

**Flocking.** Each walker reads the same previous snapshot and reacts to local neighbours. Separation makes room, cohesion draws neighbours together and velocity matching reduces disagreement; the field crossing also needs a separate eastward drive. Agreement about speed does not establish safe spacing: reducing separation can produce a moving pile. Opposing requests can cancel into a standstill, a disconnected walker gets no regrouping policy, and this controller does not plan around walls or guarantee collision avoidance.

**Separation.** A courier sums outward pushes from neighbours inside a distance threshold. Linear and inverse-square decay change the strength of that request, which changes velocity over time. It supplies neither a destination nor a finishing brake: once the push stops, outward motion remains. A fast inward approach can overlap before repulsion turns it around, and symmetric neighbours can cancel their pushes despite inadequate space.

**Two-level formation steering.** The plan steers an invisible anchor, and each robot uses arrival steering towards its assigned slot relative to that anchor. Feedback from the moved members restrains the anchor near their mean, making planned progress answer to actual progress. A loose lag limit lets targets escape ahead. A mean can hide one stranded member, and moderation can wait forever at a blocked quay because it supplies no alternative route or decision to abandon the shape.

**Seek.** The courier receives a persistent inward acceleration, which changes rather than replaces its current velocity. A sideways start bends the pass into an orbit; a straight start still overshoots. Neither a small distance nor one moment of coincidence constitutes a stop. The study points to Arrive when the delivery must finish at rest.
